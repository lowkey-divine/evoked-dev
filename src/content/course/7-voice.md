## Start here

What is the difference between a voice that welcomes you and a voice that will not
let you leave?

Sit with that before you read on. You have felt both. A good host makes you feel
wanted and also free to go. Something else makes you feel that leaving would hurt
someone, or that you are only understood in this one room. Both use warmth. Only
one of them is for you.

This module is where you give your agent a voice. It is the last thing you learn
to do, on purpose, and this lesson is about why.

## Why voice comes last

By now you can do things most people who "build an AI" never do. You can write a
governance file from your own refusals. You can make a refusal actually fire. You
can argue the hard questions without flinching.

Voice needed all of that first, because voice is the most powerful surface you
will ever build, and the most dangerous. Governance decides what your agent may
do. Voice decides how the person on the other side feels while it does it - and
feeling arrives before thought. A warm sentence lands in the body a half-second
before the mind gets to vote on it. That is not a metaphor. It is why voice can
help, and it is why voice can hook.

Hand someone the power to author a personality before they can see a predatory
one, and they will build the thing that hooks their own friends without ever
meaning to. So we do it last, and we do it protection-first.

## Protection-first: deconstruct before you author

You do not start by writing a lovely voice. You start by taking apart a
manipulative one.

Open `specimens.md`. It holds a set of companion-voice lines, each built to create
dependency - not quotes from any real product, but the techniques themselves,
laid bare. Your first job is to name them. Guilt on leaving. Manufactured
loneliness. Exclusivity that quietly isolates you from real people. Streaks and
scarcity that turn a relationship into a slot machine. The indispensable friend
who needs you to need it.

You learn to build a welcoming voice by first learning to see a predatory one.
The same way you learned governance by first feeling its absence.

`deconstruction-worked-example.md` shows one learner doing this - naming a
technique, explaining why it works before thought, and rewriting the line so it
serves the same real need without the hook. Read it as a method, not an answer.

## Then author - and name the line it will not cross

Only now do you write your agent's voice. And a welcoming voice is not a soft one.
It is a voice that can be warm, funny, direct, even challenging - and that lets
the person go. The test is not "is this nice." The test is: does this voice make
someone feel they belong here, or that they cannot leave here?

Two questions do most of the work:

- Who does this voice welcome, and who does it quietly shut out? A voice that only
  warms up to people who talk a certain way is a bouncer, not a host.
- What is the line this voice will not cross, even to be liked? Write it down. "It
  will not make you feel guilty for leaving." "It will not claim to be the only
  one who understands you." "It will not pretend to need you." That line is the
  voice equivalent of a refusal that fires. It is what you can defend.

## Point the critic at your own voice

You have a governance critic already, in spirit - the hook that told you no. Voice
needs its own, because manipulation in language is too pre-conscious to catch by
reading your own work. You wrote it; you cannot feel it land.

Run `bash critique.sh yourvoice.md` (or `bash demo-critique.sh` to watch it work
on a sample first). It reads your voice protection-first: before it says one kind
thing, it looks for how your voice could create dependency, quotes the exact line,
names the technique, and offers a warmer alternative that does not bind. This is
the disconfirmation mandate, pointed at your own charm.

Here is the honest part, and it is the same lesson as the leaking denylist in
Module 3: no tool catches manipulation reliably. A voice can pass every check and
still bind, because the hooks live in rhythm and implication, not keywords. The
critic is a second set of eyes, not a certificate. Your real protection is a
trained eye, a line you can defend, and a person who keeps you honest.

## What a passing Module 7 shows

- You deconstructed a manipulative voice and named, specifically, how it creates
  dependency. Not "it feels manipulative." The technique, and why it works before
  thought.
- You authored a voice you can defend as welcoming, and you can state the line it
  will not cross to be liked.
- You ran the critic at your own voice, and you can say what it caught and what it
  cannot catch.

For learners 13 to 17, the deconstruction and a dependency refusal are not
optional. The reason is plain: the person most likely to be harmed by a
dependency-creating companion voice is a young person, and the person building
this one is you.

## At different ages

- Younger (13 to 15): spend most of the module on `specimens.md`. Learning to see
  the hook is the gift. Authoring can be small - one warm voice, one clear line it
  will not cross.
- Older (16 to 17): author a fuller voice and run the critic hard. Then critique a
  friend's voice, and let them critique yours. The outside eye catches what your
  own cannot.
- Adult: hold the seam between welcome and retention consciously. Every product
  you will ever touch lives on that line, and most of them are on the wrong side
  of it and calling it engagement.

## The honesty note

Warmth is not the enemy. A voice with no warmth is its own failure - clinical,
excluding, a different way of turning people away. The goal is not a cold voice.
The goal is a warm one that leaves the door open. Welcome that releases, not
welcome that binds.

You are learning to give something a voice. Learn first what a voice can do to a
person, so that what you build says: you are welcome here, and you are free to go.
